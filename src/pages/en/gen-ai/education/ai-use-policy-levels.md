## Overview

- **Each instructor needs to state clearly what AI use they permit, for each class and each assignment** (see [About the Use of AI Tools in Classes (ver. 1.0)](https://utelecon.adm.u-tokyo.ac.jp/en/docs/ai-tools-in-classes/)).
- This page offers a set of typical choices for that purpose: a classification of AI use scenarios (S1–S5), and **permitted AI use labels (T0–T5)** indicating how far along that scale AI use is permitted.
- From here on, for each class and assignment, please choose one of these or "Other" and show it to your students.

From the 2026 A Semester, you will be able to select such a label and show it to students when you post an assignment on UTOL (scheduled for introduction at the end of September).

\* Please note that communicating to students what AI use is permitted is necessary for every assignment, whether or not it is posted on UTOL.

## The University's Basic Policy

- Whether it is appropriate for students to use AI should be decided in light of the educational goals of each individual class and assignment. This varies with the learning objectives of the assignment, the form of the work submitted, and the students taking the course (their year of study, etc.)
- Therefore, the University's basic policy is that instructors should decide and give clear instructions for each individual situation and assignment. We value flexibility and room for reflecting each instructor's own thinking, and so we do not draw a uniform line
- This page and this feature do not themselves determine in which situations AI use **should** or **should not** be permitted, but some thoughts on the underlying principles are given at the end of this page
- Our basic principles have been set out for some time now, for instructors in [About the Use of AI Tools in Classes (ver. 1.0)](https://utelecon.adm.u-tokyo.ac.jp/en/docs/ai-tools-in-classes/) and for students in [Notification to Students on the Use of AI Tools in Classes (ver. 1.0)](https://utelecon.adm.u-tokyo.ac.jp/en/docs/ai-tools-in-classes-students/). This page puts into practice the following parts of those documents:
  - For instructors: "[2] Clearly state your stance as instructor on the use of generative AIs for each class and assignment."
  - For students: "[2] [Use in Classes] ... Please follow the guidelines set by your department, section, and each class instructor regarding the use of these tools."

## How to Indicate Permitted AI Use

- Even for a single assignment, there are many possible positions between a blanket ban and blanket permission. For example: "you may ask AI questions when you get stuck, but you may not copy AI output directly into your submission."
- We therefore offer a set of choices, in order to reduce ambiguity and the misunderstandings that follow from it, and to reduce the burden on instructors of indicating what they permit.
- Below we first present typical use scenarios S1–S5, and then a set of choices (T0–T5) expressing how far along that scale AI use is permitted.

### Classification of AI Use Scenarios

| Scene | Name                     | Description                                                                                                                       |
|-------|--------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| S1    | questions/research       | ask about points you do not understand<br/>look up general or background knowledge about the topic of the assignment              |
| S2    | discussion/idea-bouncing | discuss with AI, i.e. bounce ideas off it<br/>consult about the choice of topic in assignments where students set their own topic |
| S3    | feedback                 | obtain feedback on answers or reports you have written yourself                                                                   |
| S4    | drafting                 | have AI produce a draft or part of your submission, then build on it to finalize what you submit                                  |
| S5    | creative use             | various uses beyond AI as a study aid<br/>have AI output critically examined<br/>learn about the various ways and modes of working with AI  |

Examples of uses falling under each scenario:

- S1
  - ask about a point where you got stuck on a basic practice problem
  - ask about a point in the class explanation you did not understand
  - ask questions in order to acquire the background knowledge needed to follow a class
  - research a topic you are unfamiliar with
  - translate a non-native-language text you need to read for the assignment into your own language and read it
- S2
  - consult about the choice of topic or the line of argument for an essay
  - consult about goal-setting in an open-ended creative assignment
- S3
  - have AI review your work, particularly its writing and form, as in an academic essay
  - ask where your own answer went wrong
  - obtain feedback on your own answer in an exercise admitting multiple solutions
- S4
  - have AI produce a draft in an assignment whose learning goal lies in the work that follows the draft (evaluation, verification, improvement, experiment, analysis, etc.)
  - have AI generate baseline code for a programming assignment (simple code serving as the object of evaluation or the starting point for improvement)
  - have AI produce charts, tables, or data visualizations in an assignment whose main focus is the analysis and discussion that follows
- S5
  - have AI generate the claims to be refuted, in an academic essay or other essay in which students take a position and argue for it
  - critically examine AI output to learn the limitations of AI: errors, bias, (lack of) reproducibility, differences in answers depending on the prompt (lack of consistency), and so on
  - evaluate AI output by checking it against peer-reviewed literature and other sources
  - learn how to work with AI itself: writing programs that incorporate AI (APIs), working with RAG, working with agents, and so on

### Permitted AI Use Labels

- Ti means that S1 through Si are permitted
  - For example, T3 means S1 through S3, that is, up to and including questions/research, discussion/idea-bouncing, and feedback
  - Choose T5 when, in addition to permitting everything up to S4, you also permit other uses (e.g., working with or examining AI is itself part of the goal of the assignment)
- **Note:**
  - To repeat what is said in "The University's Basic Policy" above, which of these labels is appropriate should be decided in light of the learning objectives of the assignment, the form of the work submitted, and so on.
  - They are not a scale of evaluation: a higher number does not mean more advanced, let alone better

| Label        | Description                   | [AIAS](https://aiassessmentscale.com/) category |
|--------------|-------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------|
| T0           | No AI use                     | No AI                                           |
| T1           | questions/research only       | AI Planning                                     |
| T2           | T1 + discussion/idea-bouncing | AI Planning                                     |
| T3           | T2 + feedback                 | AI Collaboration                                |
| T4           | T3 + drafting                 | Full AI                                         |
| T5           | T4 + creative use             | AI Exploration                                  |
| Other        |                               |                                                 |

- If none of these fits, choose "Other" and describe what AI use you permit
- Examples:
  - Permit S1 and S3, but not S2
  - T3, but having AI translate the handouts as they are is not permitted
- When indicating a label, stating the learning objectives of the assignment (not what needs to be submitted, but what **students are supposed to gain or learn**) and adding a brief word on why that label was chosen in light of those objectives will help them understand the range of permitted use

## Beyond Indicating Permitted AI Use...

### A Message to Students

Students are of course asked to follow the rules (not to commit misconduct; AI use that has not been permitted is treated the same as any other form of misconduct). In addition:

- It is important to be aware that most of the purpose of learning (not only in your university studies, but in every setting) is to **inscribe something in your own brain and body**, not the "work product" itself
- There are of course cases in which the work product has unique value in itself, where writing, creation, invention, or discovery emerges that is worth being widely read, appreciated, or used in its own right, and aiming at such work is a good thing
- Much of the activity of learning, however, is devoted to practice in preparation for making such contributions in the future (e.g., writing an academic essay including its writing style and conventions), to learning established theories and writings, or to internalizing them (e.g., solving problems, writing programs)
- In that kind of learning, it is not at all the case that the effort is meaningless without an outcome of the sort described above: the significance and the value lie in your own growth, that is, in the **change** that has taken place in your brain, your spinal cord, your nerves, and so on
- Put plainly, this is the same thing that has always been said ("don't do what does you no good"), but being conscious of it has become extremely important now that generating the "work product" itself has become so easy
- Please do not miss the opportunity to grow (= **change**)

With that in mind, here are some general remarks on what we would like you to keep in mind for each permitted AI use label:

- S1: broaden your understanding of the subject by clearly putting your questions into words (what it is that you do not understand), and by exploring and organizing the information and background knowledge you need
- S2: after putting your own thinking into words, encounter different perspectives and counterarguments, and thereby deepen, broaden, and refine your points, hypotheses, and ideas
- S3: look back at the answers and work you have produced, judge for yourself whether the AI's comments are valid, and improve your work while choosing what to accept and what not to
- S4: rather than taking an AI-generated draft at face value, examine its accuracy, validity, structure, and so on for yourself, and select, revise, and restructure it in line with your own thinking and argument, so as to produce work **you can take full responsibility for yourself**
- S5: examine AI output critically, learn AI's characteristics and limitations, and explore and create new questions, insights, and approaches to problems

### A Message to Students Just Beginning Their University Studies

- One form of assignment that you rarely encountered up through high school, but that is very common at university, is writing a piece of some length, called a paper or an essay
- In assignments where the topic is left open, you may be left wondering what you should write about, and because there is a lot of text to produce, the temptation to get it done with AI is strong
- Remember that assignments of this kind are not meant to test whether you are able to find the predetermined "correct" answer
- They ask about profound questions the world does not yet have a clear answer for (e.g., "how can warfare be eradicated?"), and for that very reason admit various (partial) answers, which may differ from person to person
- In such an assignment, the work is worthless unless it reflects **your own thinking**
- Keep in mind that the instructor reading your essay or paper is looking to see what _you_, as a student, thought and how _you_ wrote about it
- Precisely for that reason, if you submit what AI produced (almost) as it is, the relationship of trust is damaged, to a degree not remotely comparable to copying the answer to an exercise or a quiz
- Moreover, building up partial answers to questions that have no single settled answer, and setting differing views against one another, is how researchers work in the academic world, in which you will one day play your part, too
- An assignment to write a paper or an essay is an opportunity to practice this, so please do not miss it

- Also, if you use AI on an essay assignment and hand in the result with barely a look at it, it is almost always apparent that this is what happened
- Any expert in a subject can "smell" AI-generated text, one hallmark of which is sounding more assertive than its (shallow) substance warrants
- AI detection is imperfect, but that means one has to be careful about occasional false positives; if you hand in what AI produced as it is, it will usually be evident
- We recommend that you let AI write a long piece on a topic you know very well and see how you feel about it

### A Message to Instructors

- **Why it is important to state what AI use is permitted**
  - When it is left unclear or ambiguous whether AI use is permitted, unfairness can arise between students who are restrained about using AI (some in light of their learning goals, others out of fear that even minimal use will be treated as misconduct) and students who are not. This situation itself is a source of anxiety for students
  - Our [survey](https://sites.google.com/g.ecc.u-tokyo.ac.jp/survey-ai-in-the-classroom/), conducted since 2026, shows that whether AI use is permitted often goes unstated, and that this leaves students with the kind of anxiety described above
  - Whether your policy is restrictive or permissive, it is important to **state it clearly**. This helps create an environment in which students can learn and work on their assignments without needless anxiety

- **On detecting misuse**
  - Having indicated a rule (the range of permitted use) for an assignment, you may well wonder how improper use that departs from it is to be detected
  - There are tools out there that claim to detect whether something was generated by AI, and services that embed watermarks are appearing as well, but there are false positives and they can easily be evaded; this seems likely to become an unproductive cat-and-mouse game
  - Still harder to detect is use that never becomes part of the submission at all: asking questions, bouncing ideas, having distributed materials summarized, and so on
  - The important point is that **the impossibility of perfect cheat detection does not mean that there is no value of a rule**. Without a rule, there is no way even to define what counts as misconduct in the first place
  - Equally important is that **the main purpose of stating a rule is not to define misconduct**
  - As the [survey](https://sites.google.com/g.ecc.u-tokyo.ac.jp/survey-ai-in-the-classroom/) mentioned above shows, the main purpose is to create an environment in which the (great majority of) students who have no intention of cheating can learn without anxiety

- **On assessment**
  - Once we acknowledge that misuse cannot be detected perfectly, the next question is how to ensure the soundness of assessment
  - A practical guideline is to use forms of assignment and methods of assessment that naturally foster a sense of responsibility for one's own work
  - First, **rather than relying solely on a report read by the instructor alone**, consider incorporating the following to the extent feasible given the burden on students and instructors:
    1. presentation formats (with oral questions and answers)
    1. short/light presentations or progress reports throughout the course
    1. peer review among students
    1. (where appropriate) making the work public
  - For small assignments and quizzes that check basic skills or comprehension, one option worth considering is not to assess them but to give them as self-study material, making sure skills that have to be mastered will be tested later (in the final examination, for instance)
  - Even so, having students submit such small assignments is still useful for pacing and encouraging participation in the class

- **Things to consider in setting the range of permitted AI use**
  - Our basic policy is that the appropriate range of permitted AI use should be decided in light of the educational goals of each individual class and assignment; here we offer some guidance on how that decision might be made
  - Instructors and students alike share concerns about the negative effects of over-reliance on AI (loss of the capacity for critical thinking, loss of motivation to learn, and so on) ([survey](https://sites.google.com/g.ecc.u-tokyo.ac.jp/survey-ai-in-the-classroom/))
  - If the principal goal of learning is "growth (change) in preparation for future contributions," a natural question is: **wouldn't it be better not to use AI at all (to do everything oneself)?**
  - Here we need to face squarely the reality of learning without AI: that "almost every student gets stuck somewhere" and that "students do not have enough opportunity or time to ask questions even when they want to," and that learning opportunities are sometimes lost as a result
  - However much instructors welcome questions and answer them earnestly, there is a limit to the time available; there are therefore questions that never get asked, and students get stuck there and lose the learning opportunities that lie beyond. We believe this happens often
  - At such moments, AI, which can be asked questions at any time, for as long as one likes, and which returns feedback immediately, is useful in many situations as a tool for moving learning forward
  - Even before AI, especially on elementary and basic material, it was common to look at a worked solution instead of struggling for too long, and to learn through repetition; AI use seems to have a similar (or equal or greater) effect
  - AI can also point out where the student's approach went wrong, suggest a better or alternative approach, and supply the prerequisite knowledge the problem assumes
  - It is important to take these points into account when setting the range of permitted AI use. That is, the primary goal in setting an assignment is to **enhance students' learning**, not to **assess students**
  - When a student gets stuck on an assignment, do you want them to find their own way out of **that particular difficulty**? Or would you rather they leave it behind for now, reach the material that appears further down the road and spend time there? **Please decide the range of permitted AI use based on what you want students to experience in the assignment.**

- **On rethinking the content of assignments themselves**
  - According to the ([survey](https://sites.google.com/g.ecc.u-tokyo.ac.jp/survey-ai-in-the-classroom/)), nearly 60% of the instructors who responded said that they "feel a need to substantially rethink existing course content and forms of assessment, now that AI has to be assumed"
  - We take this to mean that many instructors are asking themselves not only about the immediate problem, that assignments easily solvable by AI no longer work for assessment, but about a deeper question: if knowledge in the sense of a body of methods and know-how can be externalized to AI, what knowledge and abilities should human beings then acquire?
  - Those would presumably include the capacity to scrutinize the claims of AI (and of other people), such as critical thinking and the ability to verify the truth and reliability of information for oneself; the integrative judgment of what to choose in light of the various facts and opinions available, AI output among them; responsibility for that judgment; and the ethical sense that underpins it
  - It goes without saying that actually putting these into practice requires having mastered the fundamentals of a field and having experienced studying it deeply

In a world that has to assume the existence of powerful AI, what to change in education and learning, what not to change, and how to change it are questions to which no one has yet provided a model answer. It is a question we should keep exploring by bringing together the wisdom of instructors with deep expertise in their individual fields. We intend to continue thinking about this and to create venues for sharing good examples of practice and for continuing the discussion.
