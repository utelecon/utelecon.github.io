## Introduction

AI is a useful tool, but there are risks: using it for unfair purposes, using it wrongly, using it too much. Here we introduce general and ethical points worth knowing when using generative AI. In short, it comes down to "do not use it irresponsibly" = "use it responsibly", and for that reason this is often called **"responsible AI use"**. Below we set out what you should know in order to use AI responsibly, and what is becoming a problem in society at large.

## Falsehoods and Inaccurate Information (Hallucination)

As is well known, AI sometimes fabricates false information and produces it with an air of confidence. This is called hallucination. Also, some services in particular tend to go along with the user's claims and tone, which makes the conclusions reached in a conversation easy to doubt. This is called sycophancy.

Generally speaking, therefore, considerable care is needed before having AI produce output whose truth you cannot verify or scrutinize, and then taking it at face value. This was no different before AI, but with AI there is a distinctive problem: the peripheral evidence by which one judges the credibility of information written by a human being is absent (the source, meaning the organization or the author; third-party opinions; whether it has been peer-reviewed by experts; the author's track record and the tendency of their past writing; the style if it is written; the tone of voice and facial expression if it is spoken), and so it is hard to tell plausible information from fabrication.

That said, obtaining corroboration for every AI output is in practice difficult. In the first place, it is not as if people had understood all of the information they obtain from sources other than AI, and obtained independent corroboration for it. When researching something you did not previously know, you will often judge the credibility of the information using the peripheral evidence described above.

Given that, what really needs care is not so much becoming suspicious at the point where you receive AI output, but rather, at the point where you incorporate it into your own document and send it out under your own name, understanding **the principle that responsibility for the information and work AI produces lies with the user (the principle of user responsibility)**. That is, if you submit AI output containing nonsense as the result of your research, the one who bears responsibility for that nonsense is the user of the AI, not the AI's manufacturer (product liability).

There have been cases in the world at large where a research report generated by AI was delivered to a client and many of the works listed in the references did not exist, and this has become a problem.

## Bias

AI learns mainly from large volumes of data that exist on the internet. The distribution of that training data is skewed, and this carries over into the output.

- For example, since much of the training data is from the English-speaking world, the assumptions and examples of that world tend to become the default
- Views that appear frequently in the data, that is, majority views, get presented, and important minority views can become invisible
- On political topics and the like, adjustments may have been made by the country or the company that developed the AI

These are in many cases not outright falsehoods or inaccuracies, and it is often difficult to corroborate (or refute) any individual output. In this context, "responsible AI use" means bearing such biases and tendencies in mind when you make AI output the basis of a decision.

## Copyright and Other Infringements of Rights

If you have AI design a character, for instance, there is a real chance that something resembling an existing character will come out. It is also possible to have it generate a picture imitating an artist's style by instructing it to "make this photograph into a picture in the style of ○○ (artist)". In this way there is a possibility of infringing copyright, and care is needed particularly when using image-generating AI.

The principle to know is that in determining whether something constitutes copyright infringement, **whether or not it was made with generative AI is largely irrelevant** (reference: Agency for Cultural Affairs, [AI and Copyright](https://www.bunka.go.jp/seisaku/chosakuken/pdf/93903601_01.pdf), in Japanese).

Whether an image produced by generative AI infringes copyright, for instance, is judged by the same criteria as for something made by any other means — whether the work resembles another copyrighted work (similarity), and whether it was made on the basis of that work (reliance) — and if infringement is found on those criteria, the one who bears responsibility is you, the user. If you were to publish a work made with AI, for example, you would judge (just as for a work made by any other means) whether it resembles another copyrighted work too closely (similarity). As for reliance, if you instructed the AI to "draw a picture in the style of ○○ (artist)" and a picture in that artist's style duly came out, that would presumably count as reliance; and in some cases the fact that the artist's work was used in the AI's training data might serve as grounds for reliance.

Copyright infringement has always had many borderline cases that are hard to judge, but it is judged essentially within the framework of existing copyright law. Unless you intend to engage in creative work in earnest using generative AI, therefore, it is enough to keep the "principle of user responsibility" above in mind. If you do intend to engage in creative work, it is important to study copyright law thoroughly.

## The Problem of Mass Production of Low-Quality Work (AI Slop)

AI has made it easy to produce substantial "work products": long texts, reports, papers, programs. Yet almost no work product is finished the moment it is produced; normally a process intervenes before it is read by its intended readers or used by its intended users, in which another person looks it over, proofreads it, corrects it, or reviews it. Those are processes by which responsibility for the credibility and quality of the work is taken by an organization rather than an individual.

- For an internal document, for instance, it is normal to have a superior or a colleague proofread and correct it, and for a document to be published the importance of this is greater still
- In academic contexts, a process intervenes in which the society or the publisher brings in another expert, able to judge the truth and credibility of the claims made in the paper or the writing, to peer-review it
- In software development too, code review is carried out to decide whether a change submitted by a developer should be incorporated into the product that is actually used

In other words, while AI has made it easy to produce work products in quantity, this has created a situation in which the burden on the people who proofread, correct and review them increases. The term AI slop has emerged for AI work products that are of low quality. It refers not only to those containing outright falsehoods or inaccuracies, but to something broader: work that falls short of what the people proofreading, correcting and reviewing it expect.

Put this way, though, there will be objections and concerns: "is it a bad thing to get help from AI with work one is not used to?", "doesn't this pour cold water on that kind of effort?", "using AI surely doesn't lower quality or productivity". On that view, here too the real problem is not the quality of AI output as such, but the human burden (of checking) that comes with the process of taking responsibility for output produced with AI.

Consider, for example, a report that B (a beginner) writes first and E (an expert) corrects. Up to now (before AI) the process was that B took on a considerable burden in writing the first draft, and E corrected it. There, even if the quality of the first draft was somewhat poor, the burden was divided appropriately (indeed B's burden would normally be the greater), and E corrected it with B's future growth in mind as well. Now that a first draft can be generated with the greatest of ease, if B sends that straight on to E, we reach a situation in which B cannot be said to be carrying their part of a responsibility that ought to be shared. B too should do their own best at taking responsibility for AI output, and it is important that expectations be aligned on this.

In academic contexts, most societies and journals currently do not accept AI as an author. Disclosure of AI use is normally required, and failing to do so can amount to research misconduct. Here too the problem at bottom is that production becomes very easy with AI, that submissions to many societies have therefore risen sharply, and that this greatly increases the burden on the reviewers who check quality.

In the context of software development, there are reports of cases in which dealing with large numbers of vulnerability reports apparently found using AI has led to burnout among those responsible for development.
