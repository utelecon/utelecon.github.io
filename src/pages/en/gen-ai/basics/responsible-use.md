## Introduction

AI is a useful tool, but there are risks: using it for unfair purposes, using it wrongly, relying on it too much, among others. Here we introduce general and ethical points worth knowing when using generative AI. In short, it comes down to "do not use it irresponsibly" = "use it responsibly", and for that reason this is often called **"responsible AI use"**. Below we set out what you should know in order to use AI responsibly, and what is becoming a problem in society at large.

## Falsehoods and Inaccurate Information (Hallucination)

As is well known, AI sometimes fabricates false information and produces it confidently. This is called hallucination. Also, some services in particular tend to overly go along with the user's claims and tone, which makes the conclusions reached in a conversation less trustworthy. This is called sycophancy.

Generally speaking, therefore, considerable care is needed before having AI produce output you cannot verify or scrutinize, and then taking it at face value. This principle predates AI, but a distinctive problem with AI is that the circumstantial information to judge the credibility of human-written information is lacking (the authoring person or organization; third-party opinions; whether it has been peer-reviewed by experts; the author's track record and past publication; the style of writing if it is a written document; the tone of voice and facial expression if it is spoken), and so it is hard to tell plausible information from fabrication.

That said, obtaining corroboration for every AI output is in practice difficult. Even before AI, people has not always been confirming or corroborating information, whether it is from AI or not. When researching something you did not previously know, you will often judge the credibility of the information using the peripheral evidence described above.

Given that reality, what's important is not to become suspicious about every AI outputs but to be careful and responsible when you incorporate it into your own work and output it as yours and understand **the principle that responsibility for the information and work AI produces lies with the user (the principle of user responsibility)**

There was a widely reported case where a research company delivers a report generated by AI to a client and many of the works listed in the references did not exist. This is a graphic example of irresponsible AI use.

## Bias

AI learns mainly from large volumes of data that exist on the internet. The distribution of that training data is skewed, and this carries over into the output.

- For example, since much of the training data is from the English-speaking world, the output of AI tends to reflect norms and cases of that world
- Views frequently appearing in the data or majority views get presented, and important minority views can become invisible
- On political topics and the like, adjustments may have been made by the country or the company that developed the AI

These are in many cases not outright falsehoods or inaccuracies, and it is often difficult to corroborate (or refute) any individual output. In this context, "responsible AI use" means bearing such biases and tendencies in mind when you make a decision based on AI output.

## Copyright and Other Infringements of Rights

If you have AI design a character, for instance, there is a real chance that something resembling an existing character will come out. It is also possible to generate a picture imitating an artist's style by instructing it to "make this photograph into a picture in the style of ○○ (artist)". In this way there is a possibility of infringing copyright, and care is needed particularly when using image-generating AI.

The principle to know is that in determining whether something constitutes copyright infringement, **whether or not it was made with generative AI is largely irrelevant** (reference: Agency for Cultural Affairs, [AI and Copyright](https://www.bunka.go.jp/seisaku/chosakuken/pdf/93903601_01.pdf), in Japanese).

Whether an image produced by generative AI infringes copyright, for instance, is judged by the same criteria as for something made by any other means --- whether the work resembles another copyrighted work (similarity), and whether it was made on the basis of that work (reliance) --- and if infringement is found on those criteria, the one who bears responsibility is you, the user. If you were to publish a work made with AI, for example, you would judge (just as for a work made by any other means) whether it resembles another copyrighted work too closely (similarity). As for reliance, if you instructed the AI to "draw a picture in the style of ○○ (artist)" and a picture in that artist's style came out, that would presumably count as reliance; and in some cases the fact that the artist's work was used in the AI's training data might serve as grounds for reliance.

Copyright infringement always has many borderline cases hard to judge, and infringement with AI is judged essentially within the framework of existing copyright law. Unless you intend to deeply engage in creative work using generative AI, therefore, it is enough to keep the "principle of user responsibility" above in mind. If you do intend to engage in creative work with AI, study copyright law in depth.

## The Problem of Mass Production of Low-Quality Work (AI Slop)

AI has made it easy to produce substantial "work products" such as long texts, reports, papers, programs. Yet almost no work product is finished the moment it is produced; before it is read by its intended readers or used by its intended users, another person proofreads, corrects or reviews it. Those are processes by which an organization takes responsibility for the credibility and quality of the work.

- For an internal document, for instance, it is normal to have a superior or a colleague proofread and correct it; the importance of this process is greater for a document to be published
- In academic contexts, a process called peer-review normally intervenes, in which the society or the publisher brings in another expert, able to judge the truth and credibility of the claims made in the paper or the writing
- In software development too, code review is carried out to decide whether a change submitted by a developer should be incorporated into the product that is actually used

In other words, while AI has made it easy to produce work products in quantity, this has increased the burden on the people who proofread, correct and review them. The term AI slop has emerged for AI work products that are of low quality. It refers not only to those containing outright falsehoods or inaccuracies, but to something broader: work that falls short of the expectation of the people proofreading, correcting and reviewing it.

You might think, though: "is it a bad thing to get help from AI with work one is not used to?", "doesn't this throw cold water on people's effort?", "what's wrong with using AI, when it shouldn't lower quality or productivity?". With this in mind, here too the real problem is not the quality of AI output, per se, but the burden on human who take responsibility for AI-produced output.

Consider, for example, a report that B (a beginner) writes first and E (an expert) corrects. Up to now (before AI) the process was that B took on a considerable burden in writing the first draft, and E corrected it. There, even if the quality of the first draft was somewhat poor, the burden was divided appropriately (indeed B's burden would normally be the greater), and E corrected it partly to count on the future growth of B. Now that a first draft can be generated too easily, if B sends that straight on to E, we reach a situation in which B does not carry their part of a responsibility that ought to be shared. B too should do their own best at taking responsibility for AI output, and it is important that expectations be matched between them.

In academic contexts, most societies and journals currently do not accept AI as an author. Disclosure of AI use is normally required, and failing to do so can amount to research misconduct. Here too the problem at bottom is that production becomes very easy with AI, that submissions to many societies have therefore soar, and that this greatly increases the burden on the reviewers who check and assure quality.

In the context of software development, there are reports of cases in which dealing with large numbers of vulnerability reports apparently found by AI has led to burnout among those responsible for development.
